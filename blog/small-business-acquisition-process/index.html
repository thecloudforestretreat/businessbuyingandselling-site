The Small Business Acquisition Process

A small business acquisition usually moves through ten connected stages: define the mandate, source opportunities, screen the business, value it, submit an indication or LOI, arrange financing, complete diligence, negotiate definitive documents, close, and execute the transition. Each stage should have evidence requirements and a clear stop/go decision.

1. Define the acquisition mandate before searching

  • Set the industries, geography, revenue or earnings range, owner role, capital available, return requirements, and risks you will not accept.
  • Separate the purchase budget from working capital, professional fees, lender costs, immediate repairs, inventory and post-close reserves.
  • Decide who will operate the company and which licenses, experience or personal guarantees the role may require.

2. Source and screen opportunities

  • Use consistent screening criteria across brokered, proprietary and referral opportunities.
  • Request enough information to test revenue quality, normalized earnings, customer concentration, owner dependence, lease exposure and capital needs before investing heavily in diligence.
  • Record why an opportunity advances or stops so enthusiasm does not quietly change the mandate.

3. Value, structure and finance the offer

  • Reconcile reported earnings to normalized SDE or EBITDA and test a valuation range rather than relying on one multiple.
  • Model buyer equity, senior debt, seller financing, working capital and contingent consideration together.
  • Make the LOI clear about price mechanics, structure, diligence, financing, exclusivity, confidentiality and the expected working-capital position.

4. Complete confirmatory due diligence

  • Tie financial statements to tax returns, bank activity and operating records for matched periods.
  • Review legal, tax, HR, customer, vendor, technology, environmental, insurance, property and regulatory evidence with qualified professionals.
  • Convert each material finding into a decision: accept, verify further, change price, change terms, require a remedy, or walk away.

5. Close and manage the transition

  • Use the purchase agreement, schedules and closing checklist to define exactly what transfers, what stays, and what must be true at closing.
  • Prepare employee, customer, vendor, system, cash, insurance and authority transitions before the closing date.
  • Track the first 100 days against the assumptions used to justify the acquisition.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a small business acquisition take?

Timing varies with readiness, financing, diligence and documentation. A straightforward deal can still take several months; incomplete records, lender requirements, licenses, landlord consent or unresolved findings can extend the process.

When should a buyer contact a lender?

Before signing an LOI, discuss likely eligibility, equity, collateral, documentation and timing with lenders. A financing contingency does not replace early preparation.

What is the biggest acquisition mistake?

Proceeding without explicit evidence standards. Buyers often overfocus on headline price while under-testing normalized cash flow, working capital, owner dependence, concentration and post-close capital.

When should a buyer walk away?

Walk away when material facts cannot be verified, trust breaks down, risks cannot be priced or controlled, financing no longer works, or the opportunity falls outside the acquisition mandate.
